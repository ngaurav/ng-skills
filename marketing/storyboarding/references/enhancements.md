# Enhancements

Optional structural moves. Read at Step 4, once a sequence exists.

These change the *shape* of the storyboard, not the wording of it — which is
why they are chosen before any copy is drafted. Each one spends audience
attention, so each has to earn its beat. An enhancement that does not serve
the key message is decoration, and decoration is what audiences remember
instead of the argument.

**How to use this file.** Do not present the catalog. Pick the two or three
that fit this audience, this key message, and this person's delivery style,
and offer those concretely — with an actual suggested cold open, not the
concept of one.

Tags mark where a move applies: **[talk] [board] [async] [video]**

Most moves carry more than one tag, and that is the point. A conference
icebreaker and a video cold open are the same move wearing different clothes.
When you need something for a format, read the whole file rather than only the
entries already tagged for it — the adaptation is usually obvious once you see
the move stated generally.

## Openings

### Cold open / icebreaker — [talk] [video] [board, rarely]

A single beat before the main sequence whose job is attention and connection,
not argument. It sits outside the framework deliberately.

Formats it takes: a provocative question, a surprising statistic, a short
personal story, a bold claim you spend the rest of the time earning, or —
for video — the most striking moment of the piece shown out of order.

*Earns its place when* the audience does not yet know why they should care, or
does not know you. A room that already wants the answer does not need warming
up; go straight to the recommendation.

*Costs* 15–30 seconds of a talk, 1–3 seconds of a short. In a board deck it is
usually a mistake — executives read an icebreaker as a delay.

*Do not* make it a joke unless you are certain of the room, and never make it
generic. "Raise your hand if you've ever been frustrated by X" is a cold open
the audience has seen a hundred times.

### The turn — [talk] [video] [board] [async]

State the obvious or expected view, let it sit, then break it. "Everyone knows
X. X is wrong." The setup has to be genuinely credible or the reversal lands
as a straw man.

*Earns its place when* your key message contradicts what the audience already
believes. It converts disagreement into curiosity before you present evidence.

*Costs* two beats instead of one, and fails badly if the audience never held
the view you are demolishing.

### In media res — [video] [talk]

Start in the middle of the action, then back up. Common in demos: show the
finished result first, then build to it.

*Earns its place when* the payoff is visual and the build-up is not. Nobody
watches a setup for a result they cannot picture.

## Structural

### Section divider / chapter card — [talk] [board] [async] [video]

A beat that marks a transition between framework sections.

Name it with a so-what headline, never a label. "Part 2: Implementation" tells
the audience nothing; "Three things have to change first" tells them what is
coming and why. In video these double as chapter markers.

*Earns its place* in anything over roughly ten beats, where the audience has
lost the shape of the argument and needs a landmark.

*Costs* a beat each. In a five-beat talk they are pure overhead.

### Contrast pair — [talk] [board] [async] [video]

Two adjacent beats with identical structure and opposite content: before and
after, us and them, with and without. The parallel form does the arguing.

*Earns its place when* the difference is the point and a single beat would
force the audience to hold both states in their head at once.

*Costs* nothing extra if you were going to show both anyway — this is mostly a
sequencing decision.

### Rule of three — [talk] [video] [board]

Three parallel beats, escalating. Three is the smallest number that reads as a
pattern, and the third slot is the one the audience remembers, so put your
strongest item there rather than leading with it.

*Earns its place when* you have three genuinely parallel arguments. Do not pad
to three; two strong beats beat three with a weak one.

### Objection pre-empt — [board] [async] [talk]

A beat that raises the strongest counter-argument yourself and answers it.

*Earns its place when* you know the objection is coming and you would rather
frame it than have it framed for you — which in a board setting is almost
always. Raising it first reads as confidence; being caught by it reads as
having missed it.

*Costs* a beat, and backfires if your answer is weak. If you cannot answer the
objection, the storyboard is not the problem.

### Appendix — [board] [async]

Everything cut from the main sequence, kept behind the last beat.

*Earns its place* whenever the audience might ask for detail you removed. It
lets the main sequence stay ruthless without losing the work.

*Do not* use it as a graveyard for material you could not bear to cut. If you
would not open it in the room, delete it.

## Attention

### Pattern interrupt — [talk] [video]

A deliberate break in rhythm: a change of medium, a moment of silence, a shift
in pace, stepping away from the podium.

*Earns its place* roughly every few minutes in a talk, and after any run of
same-shaped beats in video, where attention decays predictably.

*Costs* very little, which is why it is under-used. In video this is often
just a cut to b-roll over the same voiceover.

### Live demo / screen recording — [talk] [board] [video]

Show the thing working instead of claiming it works.

*Earns its place when* the claim is about an experience rather than a number.
A demo is the strongest evidence available and the most expensive to run.

*Costs* time, and risk in live settings. For a talk or board meeting, record a
backup and be ready to cut to it without commentary. In video this is not an
enhancement so much as the default medium.

### Audience poll — [talk] [board]

Ask for hands or a quick answer, then use the result in the next beat.

*Earns its place when* the result genuinely changes what you say next, or when
the room's own answer is the evidence for your point.

*Costs* 30 seconds and control of the room. Only run it if you can handle the
answer you do not want.

## Credibility and closing

### Proof beat — [talk] [board] [async] [video]

A beat that exists purely to establish that the claim is real: the customer
name, the benchmark, the raw data, the quote.

*Earns its place when* the audience's default is scepticism — a new claim, an
unfamiliar speaker, a number that sounds too good.

*Do not* scatter proof through every beat. Concentrating it into one beat is
stronger than diluting it across ten.

### Callback — [talk] [video]

Return at the end to the image, question, or story you opened with, now
answered by everything in between.

*Earns its place* whenever there was a cold open. It converts an opening
device into structure, and it is the cheapest way to make an ending feel like
an ending.

*Costs* one beat, and requires that the opening was specific enough to return
to.

### The one thing — [talk] [board] [async] [video]

A final beat carrying the key message alone. No supporting content.

*Earns its place* almost always. Most storyboards end on next steps or a thank
you and never actually state the message the whole thing was built to deliver.

If you add only one enhancement, add this one.

# Craft: headlines, language, and the finishing pass

Format-independent. Everything here applies to a slide headline, a video's
on-screen text, and a spoken line equally. Read this at Step 5 alongside the
format reference.

## One beat, one message

A beat earns its place by making exactly one point. Two points means the
audience picks one at random and misses the other.

Test: say the beat's message out loud as a single sentence. If you need "and"
to join two independent claims, it is two beats. If you need "and" to join a
claim to its evidence, it is one beat and the evidence belongs in the body.

## The so-what test

The most common failure in a headline is describing the subject instead of
stating the implication.

Ask of every headline: **so what — for this audience?** Keep asking until the
answer is something they can act on or feel.

| Descriptive | So-what |
|---|---|
| iPod has 1GB of storage | 1,000 songs in your pocket |
| Analysis of seven manufacturing options | Offshore lifts margin 20% |
| Q3 churn data | Churn doubled in the enterprise tier |
| Our new onboarding flow | New users reach value in 4 minutes, down from 30 |
| Competitive landscape | Only one competitor can match our latency |

The left column is what the beat is *about*. The right column is what it
*means*. Audiences remember meaning.

## Four qualities of a headline

Every headline carries all four:

- **So what** — the implication, not the topic. The most important one.
- **Specific** — numbers and named things beat qualifiers. "20%" beats
  "significantly". "Enterprise tier" beats "some segments".
- **Concise** — readable at a glance, before the audience starts listening
  again. If it wraps to three lines it is a body, not a headline.
- **Linked** — it sets up the next beat. Headlines that each stand alone
  produce a list; headlines that hand off produce an argument.

## Headlines tell the story

Read only the headlines, in order, out loud. That reading must convey the
whole argument with nothing else present.

This is the single highest-value check in the skill. Run it when the sequence
is first drafted and again after drafting, because headlines drift during
copy work — a sequence that passed in Step 3 routinely fails afterwards.

Common failures and what they mean:

- **It reads as a list of topics.** The headlines are descriptive. Apply the
  so-what test to each one.
- **It jumps.** A beat is missing between two that do not connect.
- **It repeats.** Two beats are making the same point; merge or cut one.
- **It ends without landing.** There is no beat carrying the key message
  itself. The conclusion is implied but never stated.

## DDR: Draft → Drain → Refine

Write in three passes. Do not try to do them at once — drafting while editing
produces short bad writing.

**Draft.** Get the point down without judging it. Completeness over quality.

**Drain.** Remove every word that is not doing work. Cut hedges ("somewhat",
"potentially", "we believe"), cut throat-clearing ("It is important to note
that"), convert passive to active, cut adjectives that a number would do
better. Expect to lose 30–50%.

**Refine.** Apply the five language principles below to what survives.

### Example

> **Draft:** It's worth noting that our analysis of the data suggests that
> there may be a significant opportunity for cost reduction if we were to
> potentially consider moving some of our manufacturing operations offshore.

> **Drain:** Moving manufacturing offshore could reduce costs significantly.

> **Refine:** Offshore manufacturing cuts unit cost 20%.

The drained version is honest but weak. The refined version commits to a
number and a verb. If you cannot produce the refined version, you do not yet
have the evidence — that is useful information, not a writing problem.

## Five language principles

**Concise.** No filler. Active voice. This is the Drain step, applied.

**Consistent.** Parallel structure across bullets — same part of speech, same
tense, same length. Inconsistent lists read as careless and make the audience
work out whether the difference is meaningful.

**Clear.** Specific over vague. Name the thing. Give the number. "Down 40%
year over year" beats "trending in the right direction". Every unexplained
acronym costs you a portion of the room.

**Punchy.** Confident verbs. "We recommend" beats "we might consider". Hedging
reads as either weak evidence or weak conviction, and the audience cannot tell
which — so they assume both.

**Purposeful.** Right register for the audience and occasion. A board deck is
not a keynote is not a demo video. Match the room's formality; do not exceed
it or fall short of it.

## Honesty

Use the visual and the number the claim actually deserves.

- Market share is a share. Do not show absolute revenue and let the audience
  infer share.
- A truncated y-axis exaggerates a difference. Truncate only when the baseline
  is genuinely meaningful, and say so.
- A window chosen because it flatters the trend is a lie with a chart on it.
  Show the range that answers the question, not the range that wins.
- Correlation charts imply causation whether or not you say the word. If you
  do not have causation, do not use the shape.

Being caught overstating once costs you the rest of the argument.

## Shared finishing checks

Run these regardless of format. Format-specific checks live in the format
reference.

**Story**
- Titles-only read tells the whole story.
- Every beat makes exactly one point.
- The sequence follows the chosen framework, with no unexplained jumps.
- One beat explicitly states the key message. It is not merely implied.

**Beat level**
- Headline gives the so-what, not the subject.
- Body supports the headline and only the headline. Anything supporting a
  different point moves to that beat or gets cut.
- Every claim that needs a source has one.

**Language**
- Concise, Consistent, Clear, Punchy, Purposeful — applied, not just checked.
- No unexplained acronyms or internal shorthand.
- Numbers are specific and consistently formatted.

Report what changed. Do not narrate each check back.
